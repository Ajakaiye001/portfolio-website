Case Study · B2B SaaS · Research & Product Design

Workshop
OS

A connected operating system for automotive workshops. WorkshopOS ties the diagnostic, repair, parts, purchasing and billing workflows to a single object — the Work Order — so information stops falling through the gaps between people and tools.

Role
Product & UX design, research, prototyping
Type
Self-directed product concept, validated through research
Status
High-fidelity interactive prototype — validated, not yet usability-tested
Focus
Information architecture, workflow design, role-based experiences
01 The problem

Workshops don't fail for want of software. Work moves through too many disconnected places.

A repair begins as a technician's diagnosis, becomes a handwritten note, turns into a WhatsApp message to whoever orders parts, and only later gets typed into a spreadsheet. By the time it reaches the spreadsheet, the spreadsheet is already a second source of truth.

How I looked into it

Three informal conversations with independent workshop owners, twenty to thirty minutes each. Deliberately about how work moves, not about software — asking people what features they want produces a feature list, not an understanding of the operation.

  1. Walk me through what happens when a customer brings a car in.
  2. What happens after the mechanic finds a problem?
  3. How do you know which parts need to be ordered?
  4. How does the information eventually become an invoice?

What the research surfaced

Owner 1

The synchronisation problem

Ran the shop on spreadsheets, paper, calls and messaging. He didn't call it a software problem — he described it as constantly having to keep people updated. The pain wasn't recording information. It was keeping it synchronised.

Owner 2

The parts bottleneck

Diagnosis goes to the manager, the manager checks stock, stock goes to a supplier, the supplier is ordered from, and the technician waits. Every hop added delay and risk — the wrong part, a duplicate order, or losing track of which vehicle it was even for.

Owner 3 · counterexample

Already organised

This one was disconfirming evidence. It killed the assumption that every workshop is drowning in spreadsheets. Their systems worked perfectly well. They were just separate.

The disconfirming evidence reframed the thesis — from "workshops need to get rid of spreadsheets" to "workshops need a connected operational system that reduces the handoffs between diagnosis, repair, parts and billing."
02 My thinking

Everything hangs off whatever you put at the centre.

If the handoffs are the problem, the architecture has to be built around whatever the handoffs are about. I evaluated three candidates for the central object, because that choice decides every screen that follows.

Considered

Customer-centred

Good for CRM. Doesn't reflect what happens on the floor — a customer isn't a unit of work.

Considered

Vehicle-centred

Good for the technical side and service history. Misses the operational work happening around the vehicle.

Chosen

Work Order-centred

A customer owns many vehicles; a vehicle visits many times. The Work Order is the specific piece of work happening now.

The Work Order is the natural point where people, information, resources and money converge. So that is the operational source of truth: parts attach to it, labour attaches to it, the diagnosis attaches to it, and the invoice is assembled from it rather than re-entered somewhere else.

The decision I reversed

The first concept made the admin dashboard primary, with the technician workflow as a section inside it. It failed, and it failed for a reason worth keeping: admin and technician hold different mental models. A manager asks "what's happening across the workshop?" A technician asks "what's happening with this vehicle?" One screen cannot lead with both.

So I reversed it into role-specific experiences sitting on one shared data layer — which is what led to the role and permission model.

Principle

The technician should work on the vehicle, not manage the business.

The technician view is deliberately stripped down. It carries jobs, diagnostics, repair recommendations, parts requests, notes, photos and status. It does not carry revenue, staff management, payroll or global settings — not because those are hidden for security, but because they are not that person's job and every one of them is a thing to scroll past while holding a torch.

The view answers five questions and stops:

  1. What vehicle am I on?
  2. What's wrong?
  3. What needs repairing?
  4. What parts do I need?
  5. What's next?
03 The business

Every handoff costs time, risks an error, and slows the path to payment.

The target is independent mechanics, repair shops and multi-bay workshops currently stitching together a diagnostic tool, paper, phone calls, spreadsheets and separate accounting software. The commercial thesis is narrow on purpose: connecting those steps is worth paying for, because the gaps between them are where the money leaks.

It has to serve the business without working against the user. The owner gets oversight; the technician gets speed. Neither is sacrificed for the other, which is the failure mode of most software sold to an owner and used by a floor.

One deliberate restraint: AI is not the headline. The value proposition is workflow connectivity, and dressing it as an AI product would have made the pitch fashionable and the claim weaker.

04 The users

Seven roles, mapped to the jobs the research actually surfaced.

The interface adapts per role. This is not a persona paragraph added for form's sake — the two mental models found in the research, across-the-workshop and this-vehicle, are the reason role-specific experiences exist at all.

Owner

Money, people, throughput. Everything.

Workshop Manager

The floor: jobs, bays, technicians, parts.

Service Advisor

The customer conversation and the estimate.

Parts Manager

Stock, suppliers, purchase orders, goods in.

Technician

Assigned jobs, diagnostics, parts requests, notes.

Accountant

Invoicing, payments, VAT, financial reporting.

Receptionist

Bookings, check-in, keys, phones.

Customer

Where is my car, what does it cost, approve, pay.

05 The constraints

The biggest constraint was the hardware I could not build.

A real build needs diagnostic device integration, vehicle communication protocols and manufacturer data. Building any of that would have pulled the work away from the question I was actually trying to answer. So the prototype uses a simulated diagnostic integration: it demonstrates the flow end to end without pretending to talk to a physical device.

The goal was to validate the workflow and the information architecture — not the automotive communications technology.

  • Scan
  • Fault code
  • Repair recommendation
  • Required part
  • Inventory
  • Purchase request

What I deliberately cut

Each of these is a design decision rather than an unfinished edge, and each one exists to protect the highest-friction workflow: diagnosis, repair, parts, purchasing, job completion, invoice.

  • Full accounting integration The product generates invoices. It does not try to replace an accounting platform.
  • Live supplier APIs Realistic prototype data instead. The design question was how a supplier decision is presented, not who serves the JSON.
  • A standalone customer app A responsive portal first. The immediate need was visibility and approval, and neither justifies asking a customer to install anything.
  • Advanced AI diagnostics Cut on purpose, to keep the value proposition honest.
06 Projected impact

These are targets, not results.

The product is validated as a concept but has not been usability-tested. What follows are projected targets tied to the core hypothesis — not outcomes the product has achieved, and not numbers from anybody's shop floor. Every one is stated with the research finding it comes from, so it can be argued with.

The hypothesis: the product succeeds if it reduces the number of manual handoffs required to move a vehicle from diagnosis to completed payment.
Projected target 5 → 1

Manual handoffs per job

Why

Today a diagnosis passes through technician, manager, stock check and supplier by hand (Owner 2). Connecting diagnosis to parts to purchasing collapses those hops into one system.

Projected target Same day

Time from diagnosis to parts ordered

Why

Often minutes. The request no longer waits for someone to manually translate the technician's finding into a purchasing task.

Projected target ↓

"Awaiting parts" dwell time

Why

Automatic inventory checks and supplier ETAs at the point of diagnosis remove the back-and-forth that stretches this state.

Projected target ↓

Information-sync errors

Why

Wrong or duplicate part orders and stale job status. A single Work Order source of truth removes the second-source-of-truth spreadsheet problem (Owner 1).

Projected target Faster

Invoice turnaround

Why

Parts and labour already live on the Work Order, so the invoice is assembled from existing data rather than re-entered.

These become the actual success metrics to measure in usability testing and a later pilot.

Validation plan · what's next

From assumption-driven design to observed validation.

The next step is to take the prototype back to the same three owners and run a realistic scenario rather than a demo:

A 2019 BMW arrives with an engine warning light. Diagnose it, identify the repair, request the parts, and prepare the job for invoicing.

Then observe rather than ask, because what people say about an interface and what they do with one are different data:

  • Where they hesitateAnd what information they expected to be there at that moment.
  • Whether the terminology matches theirsTheir words for the work, not mine.
  • Whether the Work Order structure matches their mental modelThe central architectural bet, tested directly.
  • Whether the technician flow is actually fasterMeasured against how they work today, not against nothing.
  • Whether managers can read job status without asking staffThe synchronisation problem, restated as a test.
  • Where the workflow still forces offline communicationEvery remaining phone call is a gap the design has not closed.

Those findings feed back into the information architecture and the interaction design.

The short version

I didn't start by designing a dashboard.

I started by understanding how a workshop operates, found the real problem was fragmented information and handoffs between people and tools, and let that reframing drive the architecture — the Work Order as the source of truth, diagnostics wired to parts and purchasing, and role-specific experiences on one connected data layer.

The result is WorkshopOS.
